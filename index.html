Day by day 日程

The plan for each day, the story behind the places, and a practical tip. B and L mean breakfast and lunch are included. The links under each story are for anyone who wants to read further.

  1. 一Day 1
    Sun 15 Nov

    Arrival, on to Shaoxing

    Dongyueli Hotel, Shaoxing (night 1 of 3)

    • SQ830 leaves Singapore at 09:20 and lands at Shanghai Pudong at 14:35.
    • Guide and driver meet us; the coach leaves at 15:45-16:00 for Shaoxing (220 km, about 3 hours).
    Why it matters: a canal city

    Shaoxing sits on the Zhedong (Eastern Zhejiang) Canal, which UNESCO lists as a component of the Grand Canal World Heritage Site: the Hangzhou Xiaoshan - Shaoxing section. The bridges, embankments and boat traffic you see over the next three days belong to that working waterway. The background essay on the canal tells the wider story.

  2. 二Day 2
    Mon 16 Nov

    East Lake, Lu Xun, Bazi Bridge, rice wine

    Dongyueli Hotel (night 2 of 3). B, L

    • East Lake (Donghu): cliffs carved out by centuries of quarrying, seen from a wupeng black-awning boat.
    • Lu Xun's former residence: the courtyard home where the writer lived before and after his years in Japan.
    • Bazi Bridge: a 13th-century Southern Song stone bridge that joins three streets and three rivers.
    • Jianhu Winery: a historic Shaoxing rice wine distillery in a Qing building, with a brewing legacy dating to 1725.
    Why it matters: Lu Xun

    Lu Xun is the pen name of Zhou Shuren, born in Shaoxing in 1881 into an educated family whose fortunes declined during his childhood. Britannica describes him as commonly considered the greatest writer in 20th-century Chinese literature, and an important critic known for sharp essays on China's traditions.

    He went to Japan to study, came home disillusioned, and in 1918 published the short story "Diary of a Madman", modelled on a story by the Russian writer Gogol. His collection Call to Arms (1923) made him the leading writer of his day. He settled in Shanghai in 1927 and died there in 1936, so his story begins in this house and ends in the city where our trip finishes.

    Read further: Britannica, Lu Xun (opens in a new tab).

    Tip

    The wine tasting is optional; the story of how it is brewed is worth hearing either way.

  3. 三Day 3
    Tue 17 Nov

    Pearl harvest and the Orchid Pavilion

    Dongyueli Hotel (night 3 of 3). B, L

    • Zhuji pearl farm harvest, processing factory, pearl village and wholesale mall.
    • Lanting (Orchid Pavilion), where the calligrapher Wang Xizhi and fellow scholars drank wine and wrote poems.
    Why it matters: freshwater pearls

    Most Chinese freshwater pearls are grown by implanting small pieces of mantle tissue into a mussel, rather than a shell bead. The main species used is the freshwater mussel Hyriopsis cumingii.

    Shanxiahu Town, part of Zhuji and about 250 km south of Shanghai, is recognised as a global capital for growing, processing and trading these pearls. At the harvest, watch for the mussels being opened and the pearls sorted by size, shape and lustre. The background essay on pearls covers how the industry rose and changed.

    Read further: GIA, Chinese freshwater pearl culture (2007) (opens in a new tab).

    Why it matters: the Orchid Pavilion

    In 353 CE, during the Spring Purification Festival, some 42 literary figures gathered at Lanting near present-day Shaoxing to compose poems. Wang Xizhi, regarded as one of China's greatest calligraphers, wrote the preface to the collection. The Lanting Xu became his most famous work and the best-known early example of running script (xingshu), the flowing hand between formal and cursive writing. The original is lost; it survives only in fine tracing copies and rubbings, which is why so many versions exist in museums.

    Read further: Britannica, Wang Xizhi (opens in a new tab); Britannica, xingshu (opens in a new tab).

    Tonight: pack the Huangshan backpack

    One night on the mountain, no suitcases. Pack clean underwear and socks, basic toiletries, warm layers, hat, neck warmer, gloves and a torch in a light backpack, so your hands are free. Borrow a hiking stick if you want one. The agency keeps the main luggage overnight.

  4. 四Day 4
    Wed 18 Nov

    Train to Huangshan, up the mountain

    Xihai Hotel South Wing, mountaintop (1 night). B, L

    • Check out; coach to Hangzhou East Station (about 1 hour).
    • Train G7313 at 10:34 to Huangshan North, arriving 12:02 (first class).
    • New guide and driver; lunch; drive to the South Gate of the scenic area.
    • Scenic-area shuttle bus (20 min) to the Yungu (Cloud Valley) cable car; 10 minutes up.
    • Walk about 2 hours to the hotel, with many viewpoints for photos along the way.
    • Optional after check-in: other trails such as the Xihai (West Sea) Grand Canyon, or a sunset hike.
    Why it matters: the mountain in the paintings

    Mount Huangshan was inscribed by UNESCO in 1990 as a mixed site, recognised for both its natural and its cultural value. UNESCO describes how its peaks rising out of a sea of clouds drew hermits, poets and landscape painters, and inspired some of the finest Chinese painting, poetry and temple architecture. It also notes that the mountain shaped the image of the Oriental landscape in the world's imagination.

    It matters for nature too. The mountain is home to plant species found only locally or nationally, several of them globally threatened. It was also named a UNESCO Global Geopark in 2004 and a Biosphere Reserve in 2018. The famous pines are protected: in 1998 UNESCO asked the authorities to take all necessary measures against pine-wilt disease.

    Read further: UNESCO, Mount Huangshan (opens in a new tab); UNESCO periodic report (opens in a new tab); UNESCO state of conservation record (opens in a new tab).

    Tip

    The organiser describes the renovated South Wing as the best hotel on the mountain, comparable to a five-star hotel. Wear shoes with good grip; steps can be wet or icy.

  5. 五Day 5
    Thu 19 Nov

    Sunrise, Lion Peak, cable car down

    Crowne Plaza Huangshan Yucheng (1 night). B, L

    • 05:30 departure for sunrise at Refreshing Terrace. Leave your belongings; we return to the hotel for breakfast.
    • Check out, then either a morning walk to Lion Peak and Dawn Pavilion for rock formations, the sea of clouds and the pines of the North Sea area, or a relaxed morning around the hotel.
    • Optional: Beginning-to-Believe Peak, overlooking Mushroom Pavilion.
    • Lunch at the hotel, then the Yungu cable car down.
    Why it matters: what you are looking at

    The mountain has been admired for well over a thousand years. UNESCO's records note that it was given the name Huangshan by imperial order in 747, during the Tang dynasty, and that its scenery first entered the Chinese imagination at that time. Ever since, it has drawn a steady stream of visitors, poets and painters. The pines growing out of bare granite, and the peaks appearing and disappearing in cloud, are the same motifs you will have seen in ink landscape scrolls. Sunrise from Refreshing Terrace is one of the classic Huangshan views, which is why the early start is worth it.

    Read further: UNESCO periodic report, second cycle (opens in a new tab); UNESCO periodic report, third cycle (opens in a new tab).

    Sunrise checklist

    The walk is about 30 minutes, roughly 700 m. The hotel can lend you a down coat. Dress warm and cover head, neck and hands; late-November snow is possible. Bring a torch: a clip-on for your cap or a head torch keeps hands free, but a hand-held one is fine.

  6. 六Day 6
    Fri 20 Nov

    Hongcun, then on to Jingdezhen

    Hyatt Place Jingdezhen Taoxichuan (night 1 of 4). B, L

    • Hongcun, the ancient village at the foot of the Yellow Mountains where Crouching Tiger, Hidden Dragon was filmed.
    • Coach to Jingdezhen (about 2.5 hours).
    • Taoxichuan designer ceramics market (Thursday to Saturday, 4-10 pm).
    Why it matters: a merchant village built around water

    Hongcun and its neighbour Xidi, in Yi County, were inscribed by UNESCO in 2000 as the Ancient Villages in Southern Anhui. They were built by officials and merchants who made their fortunes elsewhere and came home to build. Their society was organised around family and clan, and their wealth came from trade.

    UNESCO singles out three things: the street plan, the architecture and decoration, and the way houses are joined to a complete water system. Traditional rural settlements of this kind largely disappeared or changed in China over the past century; here they survive to a remarkable degree. As you walk, notice the channels running past doorways and the carved decoration on merchant houses. The region's traditional Huizhou timber construction techniques are themselves recognised as intangible cultural heritage, according to UNESCO's report on nearby Huangshan.

    Read further: UNESCO, Xidi and Hongcun (opens in a new tab); UNESCO inscription decision (2000) (opens in a new tab); UNESCO periodic report, Mount Huangshan (opens in a new tab).

  7. 七Day 7
    Sat 21 Nov

    Markets, studios and the China Porcelain Museum

    Hyatt Place Jingdezhen (night 2 of 4). B, L

    • Sculpture Park creative ceramics market (Saturday mornings only), where young artists sell original work.
    • Taoxichuan Art District: the former Universal Porcelain Factory, China's largest ceramic exporter to the US in 1949-78, now an award-winning regeneration project.
    • Sanbao International Ceramic Art Village: a countryside colony of studios and galleries.
    • China Porcelain Museum: about 2,000 exhibits across Jingdezhen's history. Passport required.
    Why it matters: the porcelain capital

    Jingdezhen began as a market town called Changnanzhen and took its present name in 1004, the first year of the Song emperor's Jingde reign. By the late 12th century it was exporting porcelain in great quantities. Under the Ming (1368-1644) it produced high-quality ware on a vast scale for the imperial household and the government.

    Under the Qing, the state founded a vast ceramics factory here, the first time one can properly speak of "imperial kilns". Britannica names three great directors of the factory, among them Zang Yingxuan (1682-1700) and Nian Xiyao (1726-36). In China the city's name is still almost a synonym for porcelain.

    Read further: Britannica, Jingdezhen (opens in a new tab).

  8. 八Day 8
    Sun 22 Nov

    Imperial kilns

    Hyatt Place Jingdezhen (night 3 of 4). B, L

    • Imperial Kiln Museum, beside the ruins of the Ming and Qing imperial kilns. Passport required.
    • Ancient Kiln & Folk Customs Museum: an outdoor park of old kilns and workshops.
    • Free time for shopping in Taoxichuan.
    Why it matters: from Jingdezhen to the world

    Chinese porcelain reached Europe in the 14th century as a great luxury; early pieces were often set in gilt silver mounts. After Portugal opened sea routes to Asia in the early 16th century, Chinese potters began making wares for the export market. One early ewer carries the Portuguese royal arms painted upside down, because the painters did not know the symbol.

    Blue-and-white dishes became known in Europe as kraak porcelain, after the Dutch word for the Portuguese carrack ships. On the voyage, porcelain was packed at the lowest level of the hold, serving as ballast because water could not harm it, with the more valuable tea stored above. Underglaze blue dominated the export trade until the end of the 17th century.

    Read further: The Met, Chinese export porcelain (opens in a new tab).

  9. 九Day 9
    Mon 23 Nov

    Ceramic Palace, Gaoling and Yaoli

    Hyatt Place Jingdezhen (night 4 of 4). B, L

    • Xinping Ceramic Palace: built by Yu Ermei, who started at 80, from about 60,000 pieces of porcelain and 80 tonnes of shards.
    • Dongbu Ancient Wharf: a Ming and Qing water-transport hub that shipped clay to the kilns.
    • Gaoling Mountain's Reunion Pavilion, a 14th-century midpoint between the mines and the village.
    • Yaoli Ancient Town, with remains of 127 mining pits, 67 kiln sites and 600 workshops.
    • Fuliang Ancient Government Office, a county office dating to the Tang dynasty.
    Why it matters: the word kaolin

    Gaoling village, in Ehu Town, Fuliang County, gave its name to two geological terms: kaolin clay and kaolin stone. Kaolin is the white clay that makes true hard-paste porcelain possible. It was mined at Gaoling for more than 1,200 years, from the Southern and Northern Dynasties until 1969, when the deposit was exhausted.

    Today's route traces the supply chain behind the porcelain you saw on Days 7 and 8: clay dug at Gaoling, carried by water through wharves such as Dongbu, and fired in Jingdezhen's kilns.

    Read further: Springer, Dictionary of Geotourism: Gaolingcun Kaolin Deposit (opens in a new tab).

    Tonight

    Pack for an early start: the train leaves at 08:47. Wrap porcelain purchases well.

  11. 十一Day 11
    Wed 25 Nov

    Yu Garden, Jade Buddha, the Bund

    Renaissance Shanghai Yu Garden (night 2 of 2). B, L

    • Yuyuan Garden: a 16th-century Ming private garden of pavilions, ponds and corridors.
    • Jade Buddha Temple: two Buddha statues from Burma, each carved from a single block of white jade.
    • The Bund: about 1.5 km of European-style former banks and trading houses.
    • Nanjing Road: from long-established Chinese brands to international boutiques.
    • Farewell dinner.
    Why it matters: the Bund

    Shanghai was one of the first Chinese ports opened to Western trade, and it long dominated the nation's commerce. The Bund is where that history is most visible. Shanghai's government describes it as a 1.5 km protected historic waterfront, lined by 52 Gothic and Baroque-style buildings that once housed old Shanghai's banks and trading companies.

    "Bund" means embankment. Among the buildings to look for are the Customs House and the former Hongkong and Shanghai Bank, and at the southern end, the slender round Signal Tower that once guided river traffic. Across the water is Pudong, where you stood yesterday: old and new Shanghai facing each other.

    Read further: Shanghai Municipal Education Commission, The Bund (opens in a new tab); Britannica, Shanghai (opens in a new tab); Frommer's, The Bund (opens in a new tab).

Background reading 读

The day notes explain each place. These three essays pick up the threads that run across several days, for the plane or a quiet evening.

A canal that tied an empire together 运河

The Grand Canal is a vast waterway system running from Beijing in the north to Zhejiang in the south, as far as the port of Ningbo on the East China Sea. UNESCO's evaluation explains that it links five of China's largest river basins: the Hai, the Yellow River, the Huai, the Yangtze and the Qiantang. It was built section by section over many centuries, under different dynasties, and its central purpose was the imperial grain transport system that fed the capitals in the north.

The Zhedong Canal carried the system on from Hangzhou through Shaoxing towards Ningbo and the sea. So the quiet canal streets of our first three days were once part of a national artery, linking the region's farms and workshops to the court and to ports overseas. UNESCO inscribed the whole system as a World Heritage Site in 2014.

Read further: UNESCO advisory body evaluation, The Grand Canal (opens in a new tab); UNESCO, The Grand Canal: components (opens in a new tab).

Pearls: how China came to lead the world 珍珠

For most of history, Chinese pearls came from wild harvests. According to the Gemological Institute of America, freshwater pearl culturing stayed largely stagnant from the 11th century until the 1950s. Modern tissue-nucleated culturing then took off, and by 1984 China's freshwater output passed 100 tonnes, more than Japan's entire cultured pearl production of about 64 tonnes. China has led the world ever since.

In 1992 the state dropped its unified management and export licensing for pearls. Output later fell sharply, from 5,265 tonnes in 2006 to 454 tonnes in 2020, bringing price volatility and raising questions about the industry's long-term viability. GIA's 2026 study looks at how the industry is responding, and Shanxiahu in Zhuji, which we visit on Day 3, sits at the centre of that story.

Read further: GIA, Policy-driven sustainability in China's freshwater pearl culturing industry (2026) (opens in a new tab); GIA, Continuity and change in Chinese freshwater pearl culture (2007) (opens in a new tab).

Reading blue-and-white: kraak dishes and reign marks 青花

Two things help when looking at Jingdezhen blue-and-white in a museum case. The first is the export look. The Met describes kraak dishes, the mainstay of the early export trade, as having decoration divided into panels around a wide border, with a stylised landscape in the centre. Later, European buyers sent models of forms unknown in China, such as mugs, candlesticks and tazze, for Jingdezhen to copy, and armorial services painted with family coats of arms became a defining export product of the 18th century.

The second is the base. The V&A notes that the custom of inscribing the emperor's reign title on wares began in the Ming. Christie's explains how to read one: the characters run top to bottom and right to left. In a six-character mark, the first two give the dynasty (Da Ming or Da Qing), the next two the emperor's reign name, and the last two are nian zhi. Four-character marks leave out the dynasty. But a mark is not proof. Craftsmen often copied the marks of earlier reigns out of respect for those periods, and many later copies carry faked marks. The V&A's catalogue records a copied mark on a Kangxi-period ginger jar: a mark of the earlier Chenghua reign. Expect to see both genuine imperial pieces and these subtleties in the Jingdezhen museums.

Read further: The Met, East and West: Chinese export porcelain (opens in a new tab); V&A, Ming jar from Jingdezhen (opens in a new tab); V&A, Kangxi jar with a Chenghua mark (opens in a new tab); Christie's, A guide to reign marks on Chinese ceramics (opens in a new tab).

Practical 须知

What to sort out before you fly, what to carry each day, and what makes the trip easier on the ground.

Before you fly

  • Passport valid for more than six months.
  • Entry. Singapore ordinary passports: visa-free for up to 30 days (ICA (opens in a new tab)). Many European passports, including French, are also visa-free until 31 December 2026 (Fujian Provincial Government, reporting China Daily (opens in a new tab)). Everyone should confirm the rules for their own passport.
  • China arrival card online from 72 hours before departure (organiser's note), so from Thu 12 Nov, 09:20. Fill it in on the National Immigration Administration website (opens in a new tab).
  • Alipay installed, identity verified and an international card linked. Test it before you go.
  • AMap Global (高德地图): install from the App Store or Google Play. It has had an English version since January 2025 (Shanghai Government (opens in a new tab)).
  • Travel insurance covering medical costs, emergency help and trip interruption.

Before you fly home

SG Arrival Card, from Tue 24 Nov. Everyone arriving in Singapore by air must submit it, including Singapore citizens and permanent residents, within 3 days before arrival, counting the arrival day. It is free on the ICA website (opens in a new tab) or the MyICA app; avoid paid look-alike sites.

Every morning, before you leave the hotel

  • Passport in a jacket pocket with a secure zip or button. Some museums and every train need it.
  • A folded stack of tissues from the room, or a few packets. Public toilets generally have none.
  • Small umbrella or raincoat. Rain is in the forecast.
  • Hotel name in Chinese (screenshot), phone and a charged power bank.

Money

Currency is the renminbi (RMB or CNY, ¥). The organiser's guide uses S$1 ≈ RMB 5.2 (October 2026): divide by 5 for a quick estimate. Check the rate again just before you leave.

  • Alipay or WeChat Pay are widely accepted; smaller shops and vendors often prefer them to cards.
  • The ¥200 rule. With a foreign card linked, payments of ¥200 or less carry no fee; above that, a 3% fee applies (China Briefing (opens in a new tab)). Sellers will often split a bill if asked politely.
  • Visa and Mastercard work at larger hotels and attractions, not everywhere.
  • Carry some cash as a back-up, and have at least two ways to pay.

Staying connected

Google, Gmail, WhatsApp, Instagram and Facebook may be blocked on hotel Wi-Fi and local SIMs.

  • Roaming on your home number works.
  • A China travel eSIM can also reach international apps.
  • Heavy users can rent a portable Wi-Fi router (opens in a new tab) at Changi.

Power banks

Since June 2025, China has banned power banks without the CCC (3C) mark, or from recalled batches, on domestic flights (China Daily, via The Star (opens in a new tab)). We take no domestic flights, but the organiser advises bringing only a CCC-marked power bank for the flight home from Pudong.

Power

220 V, 50 Hz. Sockets are usually two flat pins (as in Japan and the US) or three angled flat pins. Bring a universal adaptor.

What to wear

Layers you can add and remove: long sleeves, a fleece or light wool jumper, and a wind- and water-resistant jacket. Add a warmer insulated jacket for mornings, evenings and the mountain. Comfortable walking shoes with good grip and warm socks.

Shopping for porcelain

  • Watch the wrapping. If it is thin newspaper, ask for more layers and pad it with clothes.
  • A small plate can triple in size once packed, so leave room in your luggage.
  • For dinner sets or large pieces, ask about shipping or handled cartons you can carry on.

Water, toilets and health

  • Drink sealed bottled water or boiled water; hotels have kettles.
  • Many public toilets are squat-style; carry hand sanitiser as well as tissues.
  • Bring enough prescription medication, in original packaging, in your hand luggage.
  • Huangshan is about 1,800 m; altitude sickness is not expected.
